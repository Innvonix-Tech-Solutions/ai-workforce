    Why this prototype: it models the initial-release slice of the CES-LINKS platform — curriculum standards, assessments, and gradebook — as one connected data model instead of separate tools. The progress-note feature is a small, honest stand-in for "AI-assisted, human-reviewed" workflows: it generates a draft from real gradebook data client-side and requires an explicit review step before it's marked approved, rather than presenting AI output as final.

    What this is, and isn't: this is a scoped, in-memory UI concept — no backend, no auth, no persistence, no tests. It's meant to demonstrate product and interaction thinking, not a system design.

    At production scale, I'd change:
    · Standards as a versioned, shared reference table (state adoption cycles change over time) rather than strings baked into assignment records, so historical mastery data stays valid after a standards revision.
    · Every score and note change written to an append-only audit log — who changed what, when, and whether an AI draft was involved — both for FERPA-style accountability and for debugging disputed grades.
    · The AI-review step enforced server-side, not just in the UI: a note can't be marked "sent" without a reviewed_by and reviewed_at field set, so the safeguard survives a client bug or a bypassed frontend.
    · Role-based access (teacher sees their roster; admin sees the school; family portal sees only their own student) as a first-class authorization layer, not a UI filter — this is the seam most likely to leak student data across the seven stakeholder portals if it's an afterthought.
    · Mastery calculations moved from client-side averages to a scheduled job or event-driven pipeline once assignment volume grows, so the gradebook stays fast and the mastery view doesn't recompute on every load.
